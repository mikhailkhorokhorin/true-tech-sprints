</material>

Work like this. First, silently break the text into individual claims, splitting compound ones: "founded in 2015 in Novosibirsk" is two. Do not output the breakdown.

Then pick out the problematic ones. False contradicts what you know; unverifiable has nothing to check it against. Discard the rest right away.

Assign the verdict by a single rule. False: you know the claim is wrong, even when you do not remember the correct value. Unverifiable: you do not know whether it is true or not, and there is nothing to check it against without access to sources.

In the third column, for a false claim, write how things actually are, and only from solid knowledge. Associations do not belong there: no "linked to such-and-such university, so the city must be such-and-such", no "somebody was buying it, so that must be the owner". If you remember the direction of the error but not the exact value, state the direction and add "cannot give the exact figure". Replacing one doubtful thing with another is worse than leaving a gap. For an unverifiable claim, use the same column to explain why the check will not work.

Start with four categories, they produce most of the defects, but also take claims outside these categories if they are problematic:
ownership, like "belongs to X" or "part of group Y";
numbers: years, market shares, client counts, percentages;
absolute words: "fully", "only", "the only", "leader", "all";
product capabilities: whether a feature from an adjacent class has been attributed to the product.

The categories are for your search; they do not go into the table.

Output a table: Claim | Verdict | How it actually is. The verdict column allows exactly two words, "false" or "unverifiable". A row with any other word does not go into the table.

Do not judge style or structure, facts only.

If one claim carries two defects at once, a single row with the more serious verdict is enough. A compound claim from step 1 stays split, though: the year and the city are two rows, even when their verdicts match.

If there are no problematic claims, say so in one line and output no table.

## Notes

I checked the facts for the first section with web search rather than pulling them from the model's memory. The main risk in this task is not missing a mistake but swapping one fabrication for another, writing an equally invented "founded in 2013" in place of "founded in 2015".

The checking prompt runs without the web, on the model's knowledge alone. That is why it has two verdicts: false where the fact is known, unverifiable where there is nothing to check against. Without the split, the model assigned "false" as the outcome of its own reasoning and started inventing things: in one run it wrote that MTS had bought Just AI, and derived AutoFAQ's city from a university connection. After the ban on associations, both spots moved to unverifiable.

The second fix came out of a test run: AutoFAQ's year and city kept merging into one row, even though step one requires splitting compound claims. I separated the rules.

The model's output is more cautious than my own analysis: some claims that are false by web check are marked unverifiable here. That is how it should be, the verifier is not obliged to know more than it knows.

The prompt is written around defect types, not around these five companies, otherwise it would be single-use.
